Amarens

Your CBT-informed companion

This chat is guided by ideas from Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) — it helps notice the automatic thought (e.g. "I look disgusting today"), question it gently, and find a kinder, more realistic one. It remembers your conversation as you go. It is not a therapist, and it will point you to real help if things feel heavy.

Gentle movement

No calories, no burn targets — just noticing when you moved and how it felt.

A few starting points

  • Yoga and pilates — look for beginner, "gentle" or "restorative" classes rather than "burn" or "sculpt" ones.
  • A 10-minute walk counts. Movement doesn't need to be intense to matter.
  • If a workout leaves you anxious, ashamed, or counting anything — it's worth pausing and asking why.

Anorexia and bulimia, explained simply

Anorexia nervosa isn't really about food — it's often about control, fear, and a distorted sense of what your body looks like or deserves. The restriction can start small and quietly become the mind's way of managing anxiety or pain.

Bulimia nervosa often involves a cycle of restriction, then eating that feels out of control, then guilt or panic that leads to compensating for it. That cycle isn't a character flaw — it's often the nervous system trying (badly) to cope with distress.

Both are recognized mental health conditions, not a "phase" or a diet gone right — and both are treatable, with the right support.

What actually helps

Recovery approaches that tend to help most, according to psychology research, include: naming the distorted thought instead of obeying it (a CBT skill), rebuilding a neutral relationship with food and your body instead of a "fixed" one, and having people around you who don't reinforce diet culture.

That's what AMARens is trying to be a small part of: a journal to notice your patterns, an AI companion trained to gently question distorted thoughts using CBT, food guidance without numbers or restriction, and movement without punishment.

Who profits from your insecurity

A huge share of the skincare, diet, and fast-fashion industries is funded by making people — especially women and girls — feel like their bodies need fixing. Many of those products aren't necessary; the insecurity is the product being sold, and the item is just the receipt.

A small framework: Notice → Hold gently → Reach out

1

Notice

Name what's happening without judging it. "I'm restricting" or "I keep checking my body" is more useful than "I'm being weak."

2

Hold it gently

You don't have to fix it in this moment. One kind sentence to yourself is enough for now — the kind you'd say to someone you love.

3

Reach out

Tell someone — a friend, Amarens, a professional. Saying it early tends to make it lighter than carrying it alone.

Building a daily ritual

Recovery-adjacent habits tend to stick better as a small, repeatable ritual rather than a big rulebook. A simple version, using the tools already in this app:

  • Morning: one line in the Journal about how you're arriving today — no need for more than a sentence.
  • Midday: a body check without judgment — hungry, tired, tense? Just notice, don't fix.
  • Evening: log any movement (even "rest day" counts), and if something felt heavy, bring it to Amarens in the Chat tab.
  • Weekly: redo the check-in in M1 — patterns are much easier to catch early than after months of running quietly.

None of this needs to be perfect or daily forever — even doing one of these most days is enough to start noticing your own patterns instead of being run by them.

The Notebook & the Daily

Inspired by @thaaisfeijao's "caderninho" (notebook) and "daily" practice, adapted here for noticing patterns around body, food, and self-worth.

This habit is genuinely hard — the mind tends to avoid it, because looking closely at yourself is uncomfortable. It's also hard to keep up: it takes a real, honest desire to grow. You won't see results in a week; sustained over months or years, it gives real clarity. Start with once a week if you're new to it.

The fields below (gratitude, desire, actions...) are just a starting skeleton — feel free to reword, remove, or add sections until it feels like yours. What matters is the habit, not the exact template.
